Context

The Product

What Global Relay Archive does

Global Relay Archive is a compliance platform that helps financial institutions capture, search, and supervise electronic communications. Compliance administrators use it to monitor messaging across channels — ensuring their organization meets regulatory requirements and can respond to audits without friction.

The core building blocks are objects: reusable risk detection components — covering areas like keyword usage and preapproved content — that policies draw on to evaluate communications.

The Challenge

The Problem

An experience that never evolved

A compliance administrator's work requires the whole application. Establishing the right surveillance setup meant moving across multiple object types. Instead of navigating a product, users were hopping from one experience to another — relearning behaviours and workflows for every task.

Two object types, same product — inconsistent layouts, patterns, and content placement

Scope

What we were designing

Compliance administrators follow a consistent pattern — two primary tasks and a set of secondary tasks recur across every object type. The goal was to design for that pattern, but the brief needed reframing.

Reframing the Brief

From visual refresh to defined user need

The initial framing was a simple refresh. I shifted the conversation toward a defined user need: reduce the mental friction compliance administrators experience when navigating between objects.

Expected outcomes were a reusable template, development-ready designs for Keyword Lists, and full workflows adoptable by designers and engineers across the product.

Expected outcomes — reusable template, Keyword Lists designs, and system workflows

Constraints & Tradeoffs

What we were working within

  • The design system was still maturing. Strict timelines required designing with existing components. Modern patterns were deprioritised in favour of solutions that could ship.

  • Early feedback from other designers found the template restrictive. The template was made open-ended. Additional design work was done outside of scope to demonstrate what flexibility looked like in practice.

  • Backend limitations put known user needs on hold. Gaps were documented and deferred to future initiatives.

Process

Approach

Step-by-Step Process

1. Abstraction, 2. Content Mapping, 3. Application, 4. Refinement

Stage 01 — Abstraction

Finding the structure beneath the surface

Objects → policies: how risk detection components relate to compliance workflows

A content audit resulted in a clear content structure that could serve as a shared reference. This was validated with UX designers working on overlapping initiatives.

Content audit — identifying patterns and focus

From this foundation, two shared patterns emerged that would carry across every object type: the Header and Details & Metadata. These became the basis for a reusable template — a common starting point regardless of which compliance topic a designer or engineer was working in.

Content structure and emerging template

Stage 02 — Application

Testing the system with Keyword Lists

To move from abstraction to real-world application, we started with Keyword Lists — giving us specific content and user journeys to apply to the structure defined earlier.

The existing header had accumulated content over time — description, status, modified date, policy count, and additional controls — with no clear hierarchy or consistency across object types. This created noise at exactly the point users needed focus: creating and refining the object.

Header — before simplification

Simplifying the header addressed three problems directly:

  • Inconsistency across object types

  • Content that competed for user attention

  • Lack of separation between secondary information and primary actions

Header — simplified pattern with clear separation of information and actions

The information removed from the header still needed to be accessible. Customer interviews and SME sessions confirmed users wouldn't always need it, but relied on it to make decisions.

Two alternatives were explored:

  • An expandable accordion below the page title — kept content in view, but pushed the main workflow further down

  • A dedicated secondary page — relocated the problem rather than solving it

Explorations — accordion and secondary page approaches

The Side Panel keeps secondary content present without competing for attention. A content audit across all object types defined what the panel would consistently hold, making it predictable across the product — users learn the pattern once and carry it forward.

Side Panel — secondary content present without competing for attention

Stage 03 — Refinement

What the application revealed

Because content was mapped across all object types early, few structural adjustments were needed when the template was applied more broadly — the POC confirmed the system held.

Edge cases emerged where a single-page, single-workflow structure wasn't enough. Objects like Preapproved Content required tabs to support multiple workflows and a more flexible layout system to group content effectively.

Keyword Lists vs. Preapproved Content — template flexibility

Applying the template also challenged some inherited assumptions. AI Surveillance displayed subcategories and indicators as a table — but the content was purely informational with no actions. Redesigning it as readable content made the page significantly easier to scan.

AI Surveillance — informational content redesigned for readability

Some workflows were intentionally left out of scope. Policy creation and review queue management involved distinct user goals, case management complexity, and multi-step decision flows — each warranting a dedicated design review rather than an adaptation of this template.

Reflection

What I'd do differently

Setting expectations early was critical — and it still wasn't early enough. A clearer project brief from the start would have saved time when timelines and priorities needed renegotiating. I now treat the brief as a living document and revisit it with stakeholders at the beginning of each stage, not just at kickoff.

The content design phase was also underestimated. The content audit turned out to be foundational to everything that followed — but it wasn't scoped that way initially. In future projects I'd treat content structure as a first-class deliverable, not a prerequisite to the "real" design work.
